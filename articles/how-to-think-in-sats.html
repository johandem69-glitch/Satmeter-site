How to Think in Sats

Not an identity marker — a skill with one anchor, one habit, and five steps.

Live figures · recalculated … from … · by Johan Demmers

"Thinking in sats" gets talked about as an identity marker. It is more useful as a practical skill, and like most skills it comes down to a handful of anchors and one habit.

Here is the whole method. It takes about ten minutes to learn.

Step 1: learn one anchor, not a table

Everything else follows from a single number: how many sats one unit of your currency buys. Right now one dollar is …, with Bitcoin at ….

At a $100,000 Bitcoin price the anchor is beautifully round: 1,000 sats = $1. Dollars times a thousand equals sats. A $4 coffee is 4,000 sats. A $60 shop is 60,000. A $1,200 rent payment is 1.2 million.

Do not memorise a conversion table. Memorise the anchor and recompute it every few weeks, because it moves. People who try to learn tables end up with stale numbers and stop trusting the exercise.

Step 2: pick exactly one recurring purchase

This is where most people go wrong. They convert everything for two days, find it exhausting, and quit.

Pick one thing you buy weekly. Coffee, bread, a commute ticket, lunch. One. The requirements are that it repeats often enough to notice and that you already know its price without looking.

The point is not to build a dataset. It is to develop an instinct for what a satoshi is worth — and instincts come from repetition of one thing, not breadth across many.

Step 3: record three numbers, monthly

Once a month, note: the local price, the Bitcoin price, and the date. Not the sats figure — that is derived and you can always recompute it.

Why three and not one. If you only record the sats figure, you cannot tell later whether a change came from Bitcoin or from the item. Six months on you will have a series that supports whatever conclusion you were already inclined toward, which is worse than having no series at all.

Monthly, not daily. Daily noise carries no information and mostly generates anxiety. A phone note is entirely sufficient; nobody needs a spreadsheet for twelve rows a year.

Step 4: use the unit that fits the amount

One reason Bitcoin feels awkward is people force everything into one unit. Match the unit to the magnitude instead:

AmountBest unitExample
Under $1satsa 300-sat coffee refill
$1 – $1,000thousands of satsa 60,000-sat weekly shop
$1,000 – $50,000millions of sats, or BTCa 12,000,000-sat car
Above $50,000BTCa 3.2 BTC deposit

Nobody says "four hundred thousand cents" for a house, and nobody should say "0.000038 BTC" for a coffee. Using the wrong unit is the main reason Bitcoin amounts feel unwieldy, and it is entirely self-inflicted.

Step 5: know what the habit is actually for

Be clear with yourself about the purpose, because the honest version and the marketing version differ.

What it is good for: noticing recurring costs you had stopped seeing. Comparing prices across countries without arguing about exchange rates. Comparing prices across time in a currency that is losing value fast. Developing a feel for a unit you may use for decades.

What it is not good for: deciding what to buy, timing anything, or proving a point about monetary policy. A sats price is a measurement, not an argument — and it absorbs every real-world change, not just monetary ones.

One thing to watch in yourself. Small friendly numbers make things feel cheap. "Only 4,000 sats" and "$4.00" are the same amount of money, but they do not land the same way. The unit is a readability improvement, not a discount — and if you notice yourself spending more freely because the number looks smaller, the habit is working against you rather than for you.

The ten-minute version

  1. Find out what one unit of your currency is in sats. Write it down.
  2. Pick one weekly purchase. Convert it once.
  3. Set a monthly reminder to note three numbers.
  4. Recompute your anchor whenever Bitcoin moves noticeably.
  5. Check your series after a year, not after a week.

That is the entire method. Everything else written about thinking in sats is commentary on those five lines.

Frequently asked questions

How do I start thinking in sats?

Learn one anchor: how many satoshis one unit of your currency buys. At a $100,000 Bitcoin price that is 1,000 sats per dollar. Then pick a single weekly purchase and convert only that.

Should I convert every purchase into sats?

No. People who try that quit within days. One recurring purchase is enough to build the instinct, which is the actual goal.

Which three numbers should I record?

The local price, the Bitcoin price, and the date. Recording only the sats figure makes it impossible to tell later whether Bitcoin moved or the item did.

When should I use BTC instead of sats?

Match the unit to the amount. Sats for everyday spending, millions of sats or BTC for large purchases, BTC above roughly $50,000. Forcing one unit onto every magnitude is what makes Bitcoin amounts feel unwieldy.
