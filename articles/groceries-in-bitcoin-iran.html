Groceries in Bitcoin: Iran

A minimum wage worth $244 or $83, depending entirely on who is doing the converting.

Live figures · recalculated 3 September 2026 from Blockchain.info · by Johan Demmers · Published 23 August 2026 · updated 3 September 2026

Iran is the hardest page in this series to write honestly, and the reason is worth explaining before you read a single number. Iran does not have an exchange rate. It has several, they disagree by large margins, and which one you pick decides what every dollar figure on this page means.

On 23 August 2026 the free market was trading around 2,003,000 rials to the dollar. There has historically been a subsidised official rate well below that, used for favoured imports, and reporting in 2024 counted as many as eight parallel rates operating at once. On 1 January 2026 the president announced the government would stop distributing the subsidised rate at all, citing rent-seeking. So treat every conversion below as an approximation with a wide error bar, because that is what it is.

Why the exchange rate is the whole story

Take Iran’s minimum wage, raised 60% on 20 March 2026 to 166,255,500 rials a month. One wage-tracking site converts that to about $244. Iran International reported the same wage as worth roughly $90, and Shafaq News described the increase as moving workers from about $75 to about $120 a month.

Those figures are not contradictory. They are the same rial amount converted at different exchange rates. The gap between $244 and $90 is not a rounding error or a bad source; it is the country’s monetary system showing through the arithmetic. Anyone who quotes a single dollar income figure for Iran without naming the rate has not understood the problem.

The same caution applies to inflation. The central bank reported annual inflation of 53.9% for the period ending in May 2026, with food specifically at 113%. The Statistical Centre of Iran reported point-to-point inflation near 88% in July. Two state agencies, two very different numbers, same country.

Four zeros, and a currency that keeps its name

Parliament approved a redenomination in October 2025 and the president signed it into law on 22 November 2025. Four zeros come off, and the central bank has roughly two years of preparation before it takes effect, with old and new notes circulating alongside each other during a transition.

One detail gets reported wrong constantly. The currency is not being renamed the toman. It stays the rial, with a new subunit called the qiran, one hundred of which make a new rial. The toman remains what it has always been in Iran: the informal unit people actually speak in, worth ten rials, which is itself a small window into how detached the official currency has become from daily use.

Redenomination changes the labels on the notes. It does not change what they buy. Venezuela has done it three times and removed fourteen zeros; the page on this site about Venezuela explains how that worked out.

Mining, sanctions, and who actually holds the coins

Bitcoin mining has been legal in Iran since 2019, but only with a licence from the central bank plus approval from the industry ministry. Unlicensed mining gets your equipment seized. Iran has extremely cheap subsidised industrial electricity, which is the entire reason mining took hold there, though miners pay a separate higher tariff.

It has not been a smooth arrangement. Nationwide blackouts in December 2024 triggered a four-month ban on mining, with authorities blaming unauthorised operations for consuming around 2,000 megawatts. The grid operator now deprioritises miners at peak demand.

The uncomfortable part, and it belongs on the page: Chainalysis put Iran’s total crypto ecosystem at $7.78 billion in 2025, and wallet addresses linked to the Revolutionary Guard received over $3 billion that year, accounting for more than half of Iranian crypto inflows in the fourth quarter. Elliptic analysis suggests the central bank accumulated at least $507 million in USDT during 2025. Iran is a genuine case of ordinary people using crypto to protect savings, and it is also a case of a sanctioned state using it to move money. Both are true and only one of them usually gets mentioned.

Hours of work, not just sats

The seven-item basket above totals about $10.20 at Numbeo’s conversion. Against a minimum wage that is somewhere between $83 and $244 depending on the rate, that basket is somewhere between 4% and 12% of a month’s income.

That range is uselessly wide, and saying so is more informative than picking a midpoint and pretending to precision. Iran is the country in this series where the sats figure carries the least meaning, and that is worth knowing before you use it in an argument.

The honest caveat. Comparing a depreciating currency against Bitcoin over a period when Bitcoin rose will always favour Bitcoin. Bitcoin has also fallen more than 70% from its high on several occasions, and during those stretches it was the worse place to have been. Sources and method

How the satoshi figures are calculated. Nothing in the satoshi column is typed in by hand. Each one is the local price divided by Bitcoin’s current price in US dollars and multiplied by 100,000,000, because one bitcoin is one hundred million satoshis — the smallest unit defined in the Bitcoin white paper. The exchange rate is fetched from Blockchain.info and refreshed every sixty seconds, with Coinbase, Kraken and Bitstamp as fallbacks. If your browser cannot reach any of them, the figures you see are the ones calculated on 3 September 2026, when one bitcoin was $77,772.39.

Where the local prices come from. The prices in the basket are indicative national averages reviewed on 3 September 2026, not one supermarket’s shelf on one day. They vary widely by city, by shop and by season, and they are cross-checked against Numbeo's cost-of-living data for Iran, which is crowd-sourced and carries that same caveat. The calculator above exists precisely so you can replace them with the prices you actually pay.

Where the currency and inflation context comes from. Statements on this page about inflation, devaluation and the direction of the local currency are checked against World Bank and IMF consumer-price data for Iran. Figures about Bitcoin adoption are order-of-magnitude estimates from published survey and chain-analysis work, and are described as such in the text rather than quoted as precise percentages.

A note on the currency used here. Iran has more than one exchange rate in use at the same time, and the price databases this page draws on publish already-converted dollar figures without saying which rate they used. Treat every number on this page as an order of magnitude, not a measurement. This is the weakest data on the site and it is flagged rather than smoothed over.

Frequently asked questions

How many satoshis is an Iranian grocery basket?

The seven-item basket totals about $10.20 using Numbeo's dollar conversions. Because Iran has multiple exchange rates and Numbeo does not disclose which it uses, treat this as an approximation with a wide margin of error.

Why is Iran's dollar income reported so differently by different sources?

Because Iran has several exchange rates operating at once. The same minimum wage of 166,255,500 rials a month has been reported as $244, $120 and $90 by different outlets, purely because they converted at different rates.

Is Iran renaming its currency the toman?

No. The redenomination signed into law on 22 November 2025 removes four zeros but keeps the name rial, adding a new subunit called the qiran. The toman remains the informal everyday unit worth ten rials.

Is Bitcoin mining legal in Iran?

Yes, with a licence from the central bank and approval from the industry ministry. Unlicensed mining is illegal and equipment is seized. Mining was banned outright for four months after the December 2024 blackouts.
