The Bitcoin Big Mac Index

The Economist compares currencies with a burger. Do the same in satoshis and you get something a dollar price can never show you.

By Johan Demmers · Updated 12 August 2026 · 6 min read

The Big Mac Index was invented by The Economist in 1986 as a light-hearted way to compare currencies. The idea is simple: a Big Mac contains roughly the same ingredients and the same labour everywhere, so its price is a rough proxy for what money is actually worth in a given country.

That same logic works beautifully for Bitcoin — with one crucial difference. When you price a Big Mac in dollars over time, the number creeps up. When you price it in satoshis, the number tells you something dollars cannot: whether your money is gaining or losing ground against a supply-capped asset.

What a Big Mac costs in sats right now

Enter the price you actually pay above and the calculator does the rest. In the United States a Big Mac meal sits around $11.00 at the time of writing. The satoshi figure moves every minute because Bitcoin's price moves every minute — that volatility is the point, not a flaw.

The maths is deliberately simple: divide the meal price by the current Bitcoin price, then multiply by 100,000,000 to get satoshis. At a $100,000 Bitcoin price, an $11 meal is 11,000 sats. If Bitcoin doubles to $200,000, that same meal costs 5,500 sats — the burger did not get cheaper, your sats got more valuable.

Why the burger is a better yardstick than you would think

Economists take the Big Mac Index seriously enough to publish it twice a year, and for good reason. A Big Mac bundles together several things that are hard to compare across borders:

  • Local labour costs — the wages of the people who assemble it.
  • Local commodity prices — beef, wheat, dairy, lettuce.
  • Local rent — the restaurant has to pay for its floor space.
  • Local taxes and regulation — all baked into the shelf price.

A single one of those is a poor measure of an economy. All four bundled into one standardised product is surprisingly useful. That is why the index has survived four decades of criticism.

The Big Mac in sats around the world

Because a Big Mac costs wildly different amounts in different countries, its price in satoshis differs too — even though Bitcoin has one global price. This is where the exercise gets genuinely interesting.

Notice what this reveals: a Swiss Big Mac costs roughly five times as many satoshis as an Indonesian one. Bitcoin is one global price, but purchasing power is stubbornly local. Anyone claiming Bitcoin makes prices "the same everywhere" is skipping over this.

The number that actually matters: the trend

A single snapshot is a curiosity. The trend is the argument.

Roughly speaking, a US Big Mac meal has gone from costing several hundred thousand satoshis in the mid-2010s to around ten thousand today. Two forces drove that, and it is worth separating them honestly:

  1. Bitcoin appreciated enormously against the dollar. This is the dominant factor by a wide margin.
  2. The dollar price of the burger rose through ordinary inflation — which pushes the sats figure in the opposite direction, but far more slowly.

Because the first force has been much larger than the second, the sats price collapsed. But be careful with the conclusion. This is a backward-looking observation about a decade in which Bitcoin went from obscure to institutional. It is not a forecast, and the same chart would look very different if measured from a market peak.

Read this carefully: a falling sats price for groceries is often presented as proof that Bitcoin "always" gains purchasing power. That framing quietly hides every period where the opposite happened. Bitcoin has repeatedly fallen 50–80% from its highs, and during those stretches your burger got dramatically more expensive in satoshis. Nothing here is a prediction, and nothing here is financial advice.

How to use this without fooling yourself

The honest way to use a sats-denominated index is as a unit-of-account exercise, not a performance chart:

  • Pick your own local price. The default figures above are averages and will not match your city. Type in what you actually paid.
  • Compare like with like. A meal versus a standalone burger is a different measurement. Be consistent.
  • Record the date. A sats figure without a timestamp is meaningless a week later.
  • Watch both directions. If you only note the figure when it flatters Bitcoin, you have built a marketing slide, not an index.

Try it with everything else

The Big Mac is the famous one, but it is not special. Bread, coffee, a litre of milk, a cinema ticket, a monthly phone plan — all of them work the same way, and some are more useful because you buy them weekly rather than occasionally.

Frequently asked questions

How many satoshis is a Big Mac?

At a Bitcoin price of $100,000, an $11.00 Big Mac meal is about 11,000 satoshis. The exact figure changes every minute with Bitcoin's price, so use the live calculator above.

Is the Bitcoin Big Mac Index an official measure?

No. The Economist publishes the Big Mac Index in fiat currencies. Denominating it in Bitcoin is an unofficial extension used by Bitcoin communities and by tools like this one.

Why does a Big Mac cost fewer sats in Japan than in Switzerland?

Because the burger itself is cheaper in Japan in local-currency terms. Bitcoin has a single global price, so any difference in the sats figure comes entirely from local price levels, wages, rent and taxes.

Does a falling sats price mean Bitcoin is a good investment?

It means Bitcoin appreciated against that currency over the period measured. It says nothing about the future, and choosing a different start date can reverse the picture entirely. This is not investment advice.
