Groceries in Bitcoin: the United States

The baseline basket: no second exchange rate, and why that makes it the honest starting point.

Live figures · recalculated … from … · by Johan Demmers

The United States is the reference market for almost every Bitcoin price you will ever see. Bitcoin is quoted in dollars by default, the deepest markets are dollar markets, and the spot price you read on any exchange is a dollar price. That makes the American basket the cleanest possible starting point: there is no second exchange rate hiding in the calculation.

It also makes it the least dramatic. In a country with a relatively stable currency, pricing groceries in satoshis is an intellectual exercise rather than a survival strategy. That is worth saying plainly, because the same page written about Argentina or Nigeria describes something quite different.

Why the American basket is the honest baseline

Every cross-country comparison on this site ultimately routes through the dollar, because that is where Bitcoin’s liquidity lives. When you see a Nigerian or Indian basket in satoshis, the calculation is really Bitcoin-in-naira or Bitcoin-in-rupees — and those prices are themselves derived from dollar markets by arbitrage.

This has a practical consequence people rarely mention: in markets with capital controls or thin local liquidity, the local Bitcoin price can trade at a meaningful premium to the dollar price. The satoshi figure you compute from a local quote is real for someone actually buying there, but it is not a clean measure of purchasing power. In the United States that distortion is essentially absent, which is why this page is the baseline rather than the headline.

The two true statements people quote separately

American grocery inflation over the past few years has been the most-discussed economic story in the country, and it is exactly the sort of thing a satoshi denominator handles badly on its own. Between 2020 and today, US food-at-home prices rose substantially in dollar terms. Over the same window Bitcoin rose far more. Net effect: the basket got much cheaper in sats, and much more expensive in dollars, simultaneously.

Both of those statements are true, and quoting either one alone is a form of dishonesty. The dollar figure is what people actually paid at the till. The satoshi figure is what happened to one particular asset. They answer different questions, and treating the second as a rebuttal of the first is a rhetorical trick rather than an argument.

The version worth taking seriously is narrower and more interesting: for the specific subset of Americans who held Bitcoin through that period, the real cost of groceries genuinely did fall. For everyone else — the overwhelming majority — it did not, and no amount of denominating changes that.

Hours of work, not just sats

The sats figure tells you about Bitcoin. It does not tell you whether life in the United States is affordable. For that, the useful denominator is time.

US median household income is roughly $80,000 a year, which works out near $38 an hour before tax for a single full-time earner. Divide that by the basket total and you get the number that actually matters to a household: how much of a working week disappears into groceries. That figure is comparable across borders in a way that neither local prices nor satoshi prices are.

Run both calculations and you learn two different things. Sats per basket tells you about the monetary side; hours per basket tells you about the living-standards side. Confusing the two is the most common mistake in this entire genre of analysis, and it is usually made on purpose.

The honest caveat. Comparing a depreciating currency against Bitcoin over a period when Bitcoin rose will always favour Bitcoin. Bitcoin has also fallen more than 70% from its high on several occasions, and during those stretches it was the worse place to have been. Nothing here is a forecast, and nothing here is financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

How many satoshis is a US grocery basket?

At a $100,000 Bitcoin price, the eight-item basket on this page totals about $35.50, or roughly 35,500 satoshis. The live figures above use the current price.

Why is the United States the baseline for these comparisons?

Because Bitcoin's deepest markets are dollar markets, so the dollar price involves no second exchange rate. Every other country's satoshi figure is derived from dollar liquidity by arbitrage.

Did American groceries get cheaper in Bitcoin terms?

Over the past few years, yes in satoshi terms and no in dollar terms, simultaneously. Both are true. The satoshi figure only describes the experience of people who actually held Bitcoin throughout.

Is a local Bitcoin premium a problem for this calculation?

Not in the United States, where markets are deep and the premium is negligible. It matters considerably in markets with capital controls or thin liquidity.
