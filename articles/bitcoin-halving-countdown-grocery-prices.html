The only monetary policy you can read in advance, to the block — and what it does not tell you about prices.

What this does to your grocery bill: honestly, nothing directly

Here is where most halving coverage goes wrong, so let me be blunabout it.

The halving reduces new supply issuance. It does not raise the price. Those are different claims, and conflating them is the single most common error in Bitcoin commentary. Price is set by what buyers and sellers do in the market, and the halving is a fully known, fully scheduled event that every participant has had years to price in.

The efficient-market argument says a perfectly anticipated event should already be reflected in the price. The counter-argument says flows matter regardless of anticipation, because reduced sell pressure from miners is a real mechanical change. Both arguments are respectable and the historical sample size is four. Four observations cannot settle this, and anyone claiming otherwise is selling certainty they do not have.

What I am not going to tell you: that groceries will cost fewer sats after the halving. Past halvings were followed by both large rises and severe drawdowns, depending entirely on which window you measure. If someone shows you a chart proving the halving causes rallies, check whether they chose the start and end dates — they did.

The part that is genuinely interesting

Strip out the price speculation and something more durable remains: the halving is the only monetary policy in existence that you can read in advance, to the block.

Nobody can tell you what any central bank’s balance sheet will look like in 2028. Anyone can tell you Bitcoin’s issuance schedule for the next century, and verify it themselves. Whether that property is valuable is a reasonable thing to disagree about — but it is unusual, and it is the actual reason this exercise of pricing bread in satoshis is coherent at all. A fixed-supply denominator is only meaningful because the supply really is fixed.

Miner economics, the underrated angle

The group for whom the halving is unambiguously consequential is miners. Their revenue per block halves overnight while their electricity bill does not. Historically this forces less efficient operations offline, hash rate dips, and the network’s difficulty adjustment brings block times back to target within a couple of weeks.

This is the most predictable part of the whole event, and it barely gets covered because it is not a price story. It is also where transaction fees become structurally more important over time — as the subsidy shrinks toward nothing, fees have to carry security. That is the genuinely open long-term question about Bitcoin, and it is far more interesting than whether the price goes up in the next six months.

Frequently asked questions

When is the next Bitcoin halving?

Block 1,050,000 is expected around 17 April 2028, with estimates spanning roughly 16 to 20 April. The exact date moves because blocks arrive slightly faster or slower than the ten-minute target.

What will the block reward be after the next halving?

It drops from 3.125 BTC to 1.5625 BTC per block.

Does the halving make prices cheaper in satoshis?

Not directly. The halving reduces new supply issuance; it does not set the price. Past halvings were followed by both large rises and severe drawdowns depending on the window measured.

Why does the halving matter then?

Because it makes Bitcoin's issuance schedule knowable and verifiable a century ahead, which is what makes a fixed-supply denominator meaningful. It also forces real changes on miner economics.
