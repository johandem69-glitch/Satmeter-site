Groceries in Bitcoin: Nigeria

Where this stops being academic: naira devaluation, local premiums, and why many reach for stablecoins instead.

Live figures · recalculated … from … · by Johan Demmers

Nigeria is where this exercise stops being academic. The naira has lost a very large share of its value against the dollar in recent years following currency reforms and devaluations, and Nigerians have among the highest rates of grassroots crypto adoption in the world — not as speculation, but as a workaround.

Why Nigerians actually use this

In a country with a stable currency, converting bread into satoshis is a curiosity. In Nigeria it is closer to a practical measurement, for a specific and unglamorous reason: when your national currency loses value quickly, you lose the ability to compare prices across time. A loaf that cost ₦400 a few years ago and ₦1,200 now might be more expensive, or the naira might simply be smaller. The shelf price alone cannot tell you.

Denominating in something with a fixed supply restores that comparison. It is not that Bitcoin is stable — it plainly is not. It is that its supply schedule is known and unchangeable, which makes it a usable reference point even while its price swings.

This is also why the honest caveat on this page is more important, not less. Bitcoin’s volatility is severe. For someone whose entire savings buffer is one month of income, a 40% drawdown is not an inconvenience — it is a crisis. The people who most benefit from an escape from currency debasement are also the least able to absorb Bitcoin’s downside. Both things are true at once, and anyone selling only the first half is not being straight with you.

The local premium nobody mentions

There is a second complication specific to markets like Nigeria: the local Bitcoin price often carries a premium. Capital controls, banking restrictions and thin local liquidity mean Nigerians frequently pay more per Bitcoin than the international dollar quote implies.

That premium is real money. If the global price is $100,000 and the effective local price is $107,000, then every satoshi figure computed from a global quote understates what a Nigerian buyer actually pays by roughly 7%. The satoshi prices on this page are computed from international market data, so treat them as the optimistic case rather than the lived one.

Peer-to-peer markets and stablecoins have absorbed a lot of this activity precisely because they route around the friction. That is a meaningful part of the story and it complicates any simple "Bitcoin fixes this" narrative — for day-to-day purchasing power, many Nigerians reach for dollar-pegged stablecoins rather than Bitcoin, because they want the escape from the naira without the volatility.

Hours of work, not just sats

The sats figure tells you about Bitcoin. It does not tell you whether life in Nigeria is affordable. For that, the useful denominator is time.

Nigerian median earnings are far lower and highly informal, so an hourly figure is unreliable; the more telling comparison is what share of a month’s income the basket consumes. Divide that by the basket total and you get the number that actually matters to a household: how much of a working week disappears into groceries. That figure is comparable across borders in a way that neither local prices nor satoshi prices are.

Run both calculations and you learn two different things. Sats per basket tells you about the monetary side; hours per basket tells you about the living-standards side. Confusing the two is the most common mistake in this entire genre of analysis, and it is usually made on purpose.

The honest caveat. Comparing a depreciating currency against Bitcoin over a period when Bitcoin rose will always favour Bitcoin. Bitcoin has also fallen more than 70% from its high on several occasions, and during those stretches it was the worse place to have been. Nothing here is a forecast, and nothing here is financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

How many satoshis is a Nigerian grocery basket?

The eight-item basket on this page totals about ₦24,000 to ₦25,000. The live satoshi figure above is computed from Bitcoin's current naira price.

Why do Nigerians price things in Bitcoin?

Because rapid naira devaluation makes it hard to compare prices across time. A unit with a fixed, known supply restores that comparison, even though its own price is volatile.

Does the Nigerian Bitcoin price differ from the global price?

Often yes. Capital controls and thin local liquidity can create a meaningful premium, so a Nigerian buyer may pay more per Bitcoin than international quotes suggest.

Is Bitcoin a safe way to escape naira devaluation?

It removes exposure to one currency and adds exposure to a highly volatile asset. Many Nigerians use dollar-pegged stablecoins instead for exactly this reason. This is not financial advice.
