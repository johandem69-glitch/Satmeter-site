What Is a Satoshi?

One Bitcoin is a hundred million satoshis. That single design choice is why Bitcoin can work as money at all.

By Johan Demmers · Updated 12 August 2026 · 7 min read

A satoshi is the smallest unit of Bitcoin. One Bitcoin is exactly 100,000,000 satoshis — a hundred million — in the same way that one euro is 100 cents, just with six more zeros.

Almost nobody transacts in whole Bitcoin. Sats are the practical unit, and once you start using them, Bitcoin stops feeling like an exotic asset and starts behaving like ordinary money with an unusual exchange rate.

A useful shortcut: at a $100,000 Bitcoin price, 1,000 sats = $1. Hold that and you can convert most everyday amounts in your head.

Where the name comes from

Bitcoin was published in 2008 in a white paper signed Satoshi Nakamoto. Nobody knows who that was — whether one person or several, or what nationality. The identity has never been credibly established, and claims to the contrary have repeatedly collapsed under scrutiny.

The unit was named after the pseudonym by the community, not by Nakamoto. It appeared in forum usage around 2010–2011 and stuck. It is now recognised widely enough that the Unicode Consortium accepted a dedicated symbol proposal for it.

Why divisibility matters more than it sounds

The eight-decimal design was not an accident. It solves a problem that has plagued every other scarce money in history.

Consider gold. If gold became the daily unit of account, ordinary purchases would require flakes too small to handle or weigh reliably. Historically this was solved with silver coinage and paper claims — and paper claims are exactly where the trouble starts, because a claim can be issued more than once.

Bitcoin sidesteps this. Because the ledger is digital, dividing into a hundred million parts costs nothing and requires no trusted intermediary. A supply capped at 21 million coins would be useless for daily commerce; a supply capped at 21 million coins and divisible into 2.1 quadrillion satoshis is not.

The full number: 21,000,000 BTC × 100,000,000 sats = 2,100,000,000,000,000 satoshis. That is 2.1 quadrillion, or roughly 260,000 sats for every person alive today — and considerably fewer in practice, since a meaningful share of early coins are believed lost forever.

Can Bitcoin be divided further than a satoshi?

On the base layer, no. One satoshi is the smallest amount the Bitcoin protocol records, and changing that would require a consensus change across the entire network.

The Lightning Network, a payment layer built on top of Bitcoin, does handle smaller amounts internally — millisatoshis, one thousandth of a satoshi. These exist for routing fee accuracy and are rounded to whole satoshis when a payment settles on the base layer. So in practice, a satoshi remains the smallest amount you can hold.

Why "thinking in sats" changes how prices feel

There is a real psychological effect here, and it is worth naming rather than pretending it is purely rational.

"0.000038 BTC" is cognitively awkward. Too many leading zeros, no intuition for scale. "3,800 sats" reads like a price. That is the entire reason sats caught on: identical information, dramatically lower mental effort.

It also reframes the question. Bitcoin priced in dollars invites "will it go up?". A grocery list priced in sats invites "what does this buy?" — which is what money is actually for. Neither framing is complete, but the second gets far less airtime.

A caveat worth stating plainly: sats being a small, friendly number can make Bitcoin feel cheap. It is not. Buying 10,000 sats costs exactly the same as buying 0.0001 BTC. The unit changes nothing about value or risk, and Bitcoin remains highly volatile. Nothing here is financial advice.

Common questions people get wrong

Is a satoshi a different coin from Bitcoin?

No. A satoshi is a unit of Bitcoin, exactly as a cent is a unit of a euro. There is no separate "satoshi coin", and anything marketed as one is unrelated to Bitcoin.

Can I buy just a few satoshis?

Yes. Bitcoin is divisible, so you are never required to buy a whole coin. Be aware that exchange fees and network fees can dwarf very small purchases, which is a practical limit rather than a protocol one.

Do satoshis expire or get destroyed?

No. Satoshis do not expire. They can, however, become permanently unspendable if the private keys controlling them are lost — which is believed to have happened to a significant portion of early coins.

Will the satoshi ever be renamed?

There have been proposals to redenominate so that "1 bitcoin" would mean what we now call a satoshi, removing the decimals entirely. It remains a debate, not a plan, and no such change has been adopted.
