Groceries in Bitcoin: Turkey

What 60% inflation does to a price list — and why gold, not Bitcoin, still dominates here.

Live figures · recalculated … from … · by Johan Demmers

Turkey has spent several years with inflation running far above anything the developed world has seen recently, at times exceeding 60% annually. Turks responded the way people always do: they moved savings into whatever held value — gold, dollars, property, and increasingly crypto.

Turkey now has one of the highest rates of crypto ownership in the world, and it is not driven by trading culture. It is driven by the lira.

What 60% inflation actually does to a price list

Abstract inflation figures do not convey the experience. Here is what they mean concretely: at 60% annual inflation, a price roughly doubles every eighteen months. Menus get reprinted. Shops stop displaying prices. Long-term contracts become unwritable. Saving in your own currency becomes actively irrational rather than merely unattractive.

This is where a fixed-supply denominator earns its place. Not because it is stable — Bitcoin is more volatile than the lira on any given week — but because it is not being expanded by anyone. When your reference unit is itself being diluted, you lose the ability to tell whether things are getting more expensive or your money is getting smaller. That is a genuine epistemological problem, not just a financial one, and it is the strongest argument for this whole exercise.

Turks have a phrase for the resulting behaviour that translates roughly as "buy today, it will cost more tomorrow." Pricing a basket in satoshis is the analytical version of that instinct.

Gold got there first, and is still bigger

Turkey also illustrates something the Bitcoin-maximalist framing tends to skip: gold got there first, and is still bigger. Turkish households hold enormous quantities of physical gold, a habit that long predates crypto and has served the same purpose for generations. Any honest account of inflation hedging in Turkey has to acknowledge that gold, not Bitcoin, remains the dominant store of value.

What Bitcoin adds is portability and divisibility. You cannot send gold to a relative abroad in ten minutes, and you cannot spend a gram of it at a shop. Those are real advantages. But they are advantages at the margin of an existing, well-established practice — not a revolution replacing it.

A useful exercise, if you want to test the argument rather than accept it: price the same basket in grams of gold as well as in satoshis. Both are fixed-ish supply denominators. Comparing the two series against each other is far more informative than comparing either against the lira, because it isolates the question of which hard asset held up rather than the much easier question of whether hard assets beat a currency losing 60% a year.

Hours of work, not just sats

The sats figure tells you about Bitcoin. It does not tell you whether life in Turkey is affordable. For that, the useful denominator is time.

Turkish minimum wage has been raised repeatedly to chase inflation, which means nominal pay rose sharply while purchasing power did not follow at the same pace. Divide that by the basket total and you get the number that actually matters to a household: how much of a working week disappears into groceries. That figure is comparable across borders in a way that neither local prices nor satoshi prices are.

Run both calculations and you learn two different things. Sats per basket tells you about the monetary side; hours per basket tells you about the living-standards side. Confusing the two is the most common mistake in this entire genre of analysis, and it is usually made on purpose.

The honest caveat. Comparing a depreciating currency against Bitcoin over a period when Bitcoin rose will always favour Bitcoin. Bitcoin has also fallen more than 70% from its high on several occasions, and during those stretches it was the worse place to have been. Nothing here is a forecast, and nothing here is financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

How many satoshis is a Turkish grocery basket?

The eight-item basket above totals roughly ₺688. The live satoshi figure is computed from Bitcoin's current lira price.

Why is crypto ownership so high in Turkey?

Because of sustained high inflation. When a currency loses value quickly, holding savings in it becomes irrational, so households move into gold, dollars, property or crypto.

Is Bitcoin more popular than gold in Turkey?

No. Turkish households hold very large quantities of physical gold, a practice that predates crypto by generations and remains dominant. Bitcoin adds portability and divisibility at the margin.

Is Bitcoin less volatile than the Turkish lira?

Not on a weekly basis; Bitcoin usually moves more. The argument for it is different: its supply schedule is fixed and known, so it is not being diluted by anyone.
