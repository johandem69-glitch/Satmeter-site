Two things called inflation

Most arguments about this collapse because the word is doing two jobs at once.

  • Price inflation is your shopping costing more. It is measured by surveying what things cost, and it is driven by supply chains, energy, wages, harvests, tariffs and demand as much as by anything monetary.
  • Monetary inflation is expansion of the money supply. Related to the first, but not the same thing and not on the same timescale.

Bitcoin has a fixed issuance schedule, so it is genuinely immune to the second in a way no fiat currency is. It has no mechanism whatsoever for protecting you from the first. A drought that doubles the price of coffee doubles it for Bitcoin holders too.

Conflating these two is how a narrow, defensible claim gets inflated into a sweeping, indefensible one.

What the honest version of the claim looks like

Here is the strongest form I can construct that survives scrutiny:

Over the past decade, someone who held a meaningful share of savings in Bitcoin and did not sell during drawdowns would have seen their grocery basket become dramatically cheaper relative to those savings — substantially outpacing the loss of purchasing power in every major fiat currency over the same window.

Every clause in that sentence is load-bearing, and each one is where the popular version cheats:

  1. "Over the past decade" — a specific window in which Bitcoin went from obscure to institutional. Measure from a market peak instead and the picture inverts.
  2. "did not sell during drawdowns" — this is the hard part, and it is behavioural rather than financial. Holding through an 80% decline is easy to describe and extremely difficult to do.
  3. "a meaningful share of savings" — a 1% allocation, however well it performed, did not protect your grocery budget.

Strip those qualifiers away and you get the version on social media, which is not an argument but an advertisement.

Where sats denomination genuinely helps

There is one thing this measurement does better than anything else, and it is worth taking seriously.

In a currency losing value quickly, you lose the ability to compare prices across time. If bread cost 400 units three years ago and 1,200 today, you cannot tell from the shelf price whether bread got expensive or the unit got smaller. That is a real epistemological problem, and people in high-inflation countries live inside it.

Denominating in something with a fixed, verifiable supply restores the comparison. Not because Bitcoin is stable — it plainly is not — but because you know precisely how many units exist and how many will ever exist. It is a measuring stick with a known length, even though the thing it measures keeps moving.

That is a narrower claim than "Bitcoin beats inflation." It is also a much more defensible one, and it happens to be the reason this entire site is coherent rather than decorative.

Where it actively misleads

Three failure modes, all common, all worth recognising when you see them:

  • Attribution error. When a sats price falls you cannot tell whether Bitcoin rose or the item got cheaper. Indian mobile plans got cheaper in sats largely because of a telecoms price war. Egg prices swing on avian influenza. The satoshi figure absorbs everything.
  • Survivorship framing. Charts of groceries priced in sats are drawn by people who held Bitcoin. The overwhelming majority of the population did not, and for them the dollar price is the only one that ever mattered.
  • Volatility laundering. Presenting a decade-long trend hides that the path involved multiple drawdowns severe enough to wipe out anyone who needed the money at the wrong moment. For a household with one month of savings, that is not volatility — it is ruin.

The uncomfortable summary. Bitcoin has been a spectacular asset over a specific decade and is a poor short-term store of value. Both statements are true simultaneously, and which one matters to you depends entirely on your time horizon and whether you can afford to be wrong for three years. This is not financial advice, and anyone who gives you a confident answer here is guessing.

How to actually test it yourself

You do not need to trust anyone on this, including me. Record three numbers once a month: your real local basket cost, the Bitcoin price in your currency, and the date. Never two — always three, because with only the sats figure you cannot attribute a change and will end up confirming whatever you already believed.

After a year you will have something better than any commentary: your own series, from your own shop, with your own start date rather than one chosen to make a point. That is the only version of this analysis that is genuinely yours.

Frequently asked questions

Does Bitcoin protect against inflation?

It has a fixed issuance schedule, so it is immune to monetary expansion. It has no mechanism to protect you from price inflation caused by supply chains, energy, harvests or tariffs. Conflating the two turns a narrow claim into an indefensible one.

What is the strongest honest version of the claim?

That someone holding a meaningful share of savings in Bitcoin over the past decade, without selling during drawdowns, saw groceries become far cheaper relative to those savings. Every qualifier in that sentence matters.

Why can't a sats price tell me if money is losing value?

Because you cannot tell which side moved. A falling sats price could mean Bitcoin rose or the item got cheaper for unrelated reasons, like a price war or a good harvest.

How should I test this myself?

Record three numbers monthly: your real local basket price, the Bitcoin price in your currency, and the date. With all three you can attribute changes correctly; with only the sats figure you are guessing.
