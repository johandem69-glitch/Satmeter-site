Local prices are approximate and vary by city, bakery and loaf size. Satoshi figures are computed live from Bitcoin’s price in each currency.

A Swiss loaf costs several times as many satoshis as an Indian one. That gap has nothing to do with Bitcoin — there is one global Bitcoin price. It is entirely wages, rent, energy and taxes, which is exactly what makes the comparison interesting.

Why bread is the better staple to track

Coffee is more fashionable and the Big Mac is more famous, but bread has three advantages as a measuring stick:

  • Almost everyone buys it. Coffee-shop prices only exist for people who buy coffee out. Bread is close to universal.
  • It is weekly, not occasional. You will notice a change in bread long before you notice one in cinema tickets.
  • It is politically sensitive. Governments intervene in bread prices, which means the number carries information about policy as well as markets.

That third point cuts both ways, and it is worth being careful about. In countries where bread is subsidised, the shelf price is a policy decision rather than a market outcome. Converting a subsidised price into satoshis produces a real number that means something quite different from an unsubsidised one.

A cleaner comparison: if you want to compare countries fairly, compare hours of work per loaf rather than sats per loaf. Median wage divided by loaf price tells you about living standards; sats per loaf tells you about Bitcoin. Both are useful, but they answer different questions and are frequently confused.

The "loaves per Bitcoin" flip

Turn the fraction upside down and you get a number that is oddly memorable: how many loaves one whole Bitcoin buys. At a $100,000 Bitcoin price and a $2.50 loaf, that is 40,000 loaves — roughly a lifetime supply for a large family.

This framing is popular because it sounds dramatic, and it is worth treating with some suspicion for that reason. It compares an appreciated asset against a consumable, which will always flatter the asset. The same arithmetic applied to a house in 1975 produces an equally startling number, and nobody presents that as proof of anything.

The honest caveat. A falling sats price is often presented as proof that Bitcoin "always" gains purchasing power. That framing quietly hides every period where the opposite happened — and Bitcoin has fallen more than 70% from its high on several occasions. During those stretches everything on this page got dramatically more expensive in satoshis. Nothing here is a forecast, and nothing here is financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

How many satoshis is a loaf of bread?

At a $100,000 Bitcoin price a $2.50 loaf is about 2,500 satoshis. The live figure at the top of this page is computed from the current Bitcoin price.

Why does bread cost different amounts of sats in different countries?

Because bread itself costs different amounts locally. Bitcoin has one global price, so the entire difference comes from local wages, rent, energy, taxes and any subsidies.

How many loaves of bread can one Bitcoin buy?

At a $100,000 Bitcoin price and a $2.50 loaf, roughly 40,000 loaves. Treat this framing cautiously: comparing an appreciated asset to a consumable will always flatter the asset.

Is bread a better Bitcoin benchmark than coffee?

Arguably yes, because nearly everyone buys bread and buys it weekly. The complication is that bread is subsidised in some countries, which makes its price a policy decision rather than a market one.
