Groceries in Bitcoin: Argentina

A country that already thinks in a second unit — and why the exchange rate you pick changes everything.

Live figures · recalculated … from … · by Johan Demmers

Argentina has run one of the world’s longest continuous experiments in high inflation, and Argentinians have developed a sophisticated folk economics because of it: multiple parallel exchange rates, prices quoted in dollars for anything durable, and a national habit of converting everything into a harder unit almost instinctively.

Which means Argentinians were already doing what this page does. They just used dollars instead of satoshis.

A country that already thinks in a second unit

The Argentine relationship with the peso is the most instructive case study in this whole collection, because it shows what happens after decades of adaptation rather than a few years of shock.

Argentinians routinely hold multiple mental price lists: the peso price, the "blue dollar" price, and sometimes an official rate that few can access. Property is priced in dollars. Savings are held in dollars, often physically. This is not ideology — it is a rational response to a currency that has lost most of its value repeatedly within living memory.

Bitcoin slots into an existing habit rather than creating a new one, and that is precisely why Argentine adoption looks different from adoption elsewhere. The question is not "why would I use a second unit of account?" — that is settled. The question is only which second unit: physical dollars, dollar stablecoins, or Bitcoin. Each has a different trade-off between accessibility, volatility and counterparty risk, and Argentinians debate those trade-offs with more practical fluency than most Bitcoin conferences manage.

Which exchange rate? It changes everything

An important technical note on the figures above. Argentina has historically maintained several exchange rates simultaneously, with a large gap between the official rate and the parallel market rate. Which rate you use changes the satoshi figure dramatically — sometimes by a factor of two.

Because reliable live ARS pricing is genuinely difficult to source, the satoshi figures on this page are computed via a proxy currency rather than a direct peso quote. That is a real limitation and I would rather state it than hide it: treat these numbers as illustrative of the method, not as an accurate Argentine price list. For a figure you can rely on, enter your actual peso price into the main converter and select a currency you can verify locally.

This is also a broader lesson about sats-denominated comparisons in any country with a managed or multiple exchange rate. The satoshi figure inherits every distortion in the underlying local currency quote. If that quote is politically determined rather than market-determined, so is your satoshi number — and it will look precise while being nothing of the kind.

Hours of work, not just sats

The sats figure tells you about Bitcoin. It does not tell you whether life in Argentina is affordable. For that, the useful denominator is time.

Argentine formal-sector wages have repeatedly failed to keep pace with prices, so the share of income consumed by food has been rising even as nominal pay rose. Divide that by the basket total and you get the number that actually matters to a household: how much of a working week disappears into groceries. That figure is comparable across borders in a way that neither local prices nor satoshi prices are.

Run both calculations and you learn two different things. Sats per basket tells you about the monetary side; hours per basket tells you about the living-standards side. Confusing the two is the most common mistake in this entire genre of analysis, and it is usually made on purpose.

The honest caveat. Comparing a depreciating currency against Bitcoin over a period when Bitcoin rose will always favour Bitcoin. Bitcoin has also fallen more than 70% from its high on several occasions, and during those stretches it was the worse place to have been. Nothing here is a forecast, and nothing here is financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

How many satoshis is an Argentine grocery basket?

The basket above totals roughly 40,000 to 42,000 pesos at recent prices. The satoshi figure shown is computed via a proxy currency and should be treated as illustrative rather than exact.

Why is the Argentine figure less reliable than others?

Because Argentina has historically had multiple simultaneous exchange rates with large gaps between them. Which rate you use can change the result by a factor of two.

Do Argentinians actually use Bitcoin for groceries?

Argentinians widely use a second unit of account, but that is more often physical dollars or dollar-pegged stablecoins than Bitcoin, because those avoid the volatility while still escaping the peso.

What is the 'blue dollar'?

The informal parallel-market exchange rate for US dollars in Argentina, which has often diverged substantially from the official rate.
